Project 03 · Game proposal · Theme: Hallucination

Room Tone

A small web game about hearing things that are not there. You are a recording engineer, alone in the studio at 3 a.m., trying to record thirty seconds of silence.

Working title
Room Tone
Main interaction
One input: click where you heard a sound
Structure
Opening · three stages · ending · about 4 minutes
Platform
Browser · HTML Canvas + Web Audio API · headphones recommended
Designer
Ran Chen — sound, code, visuals
Context
DSC 494 · Weeks 10–12 · Fall 2026

The player is asked to do one thing: listen to an empty room and point at anything they hear. At first every sound has a source. Then the room starts playing back the session that just ended — a voice, a piano note, a cymbal — from places where nothing stands. The game ends when the player learns to stop pointing.

02

Case study: Loneliness

I played Loneliness in the browser before reading anything about it. I chose it because it uses a single input — movement — and still makes you feel something. Room Tone also has one input, so I wanted to learn how meaning can come from how the world answers a single action.

Game
Loneliness (2010; HTML5 port 2020)
Designer
Jordan Magnuson — design and programming
Music
Kevin MacLeod (credited on the Steam release)
Input
Arrow keys only · no text · a few minutes

Three moments

Moment 01 · Opening

Saw: only my own square in an empty field. No text, no instructions.

Did: started moving with the arrow keys to find out what was there.

Understood: the game sets up the feeling — alone — before it shows me any rule.

Moment 02 · Approach

Saw: a group of other squares ahead of me.

Did: moved straight at them, trying to bump into them.

Understood: they moved apart and then disappeared. Getting close is exactly what makes them go.

Moment 03 · Ignore, then leave

Saw: when I stopped chasing and just passed by, the other squares stayed on screen.

Did: after a while I quit the game myself, before reaching any ending.

Understood: they can exist near me only if I leave them alone — and the game let me decide when to stop.

Three kinds of evidence

Creator statement

The itch.io page calls it “an experimental, minimalistic microgame about loneliness” and tags it as a notgame and artgame. It lists the game as textless. In the 2020 devlog, Magnuson says he ported it to HTML5 so it would survive the end of Flash.

My observation

Other squares scatter and vanish whenever I approach, and stay whenever I ignore them. At the start I only see myself. I quit before the end, so I did not see the end screen that Magnuson changed in the 2020 port.

My interpretation

The loneliness is not other people rejecting me — it is a distance that my own movement creates. Company is possible only at a distance. Uncertain: I did not finish, so I cannot say how the creator resolves it.

03

Discussion with AI

I used Claude as a research partner. I gave it my play notes first, asked it to research the creator and check each claim against the source pages, then pushed back where its reading did not match what I experienced.

Verified research

Confirmed against the source pages: Jordan Magnuson made Loneliness as a Flash game, first released April 26, 2010. It was an official selection of the 2011 Extra Credits Innovation Awards and the subject of their “Mechanics as Metaphor” episode. He ported it to HTML5 in December 2020 so it would outlive Flash. The Steam release credits the music to Kevin MacLeod. The itch.io page tags it textless, notgame and artgame.

Could not verify: the Extra Credits episode number (two sources disagree), what exactly changed in the 2020 end screen, and whether the music reacts to the player. I list these as open, not as facts.

Two gaps I found

Gap 01

Who causes the loneliness

AI said: loneliness is passive — something other people do to the player.

My evidence: the squares only vanish when I move toward them. The cause is my own action.

Gap 02

The half it ignored

AI said: the game punishes reaching out — a reading built only on approaching.

My evidence: when I ignored them, they stayed. And they did not just retreat, they disappeared. The game also offers coexistence at a distance.

Selected exchange

Me · new evidence

You read it as rejection and said loneliness is caused by others. But when I stopped chasing them, they stayed. And they didn’t just move away — they disappeared. Reconsider.

Claude · revised reading

Reading A — rejection: reaching out drives people away, and ignoring them is just giving up.

Reading B — presence through distance: others exist only while you don’t try to reach them. Because they vanish rather than flee, it is the act of approaching that erases them — not their choice to leave.

Your observation that they stay when ignored supports B better than A.

Condensed and translated from a conversation in Chinese.

My conclusion → design decision

In Loneliness, attention erases others. Room Tone inverts this: attention creates them. Clicking a phantom makes it multiply; ignoring it lets it fade. Both games share one exit — stop reaching. And because I quit Loneliness before its ending myself, Room Tone’s ending must arrive through stillness within seconds, and be clearly marked, so players don’t leave first.

04

Narrative & concept

My reading of Hallucination. A hallucination is a perception without a source. As a recording engineer I know a quiet version of this very well: when you listen hard to an empty room or a silent track for long enough, your ears start to fill it in. You hear a voice in the noise floor, a note in the air conditioning. So my game is not about distorted images. It is about the gap between what you hear and what is actually there — and whether you should trust your ears.

The world. A small live room in a recording studio, seen from above like a floor plan. A long session just ended: the piano is still open, a drum kit, a vocal mic, music stands, cables. Before closing, an engineer always records thirty seconds of room tone — the sound of the empty room — so the editor can patch gaps later. It is the one moment in a session when the job is to record nothing.

The player’s role. You are that engineer. Your only tool is attention: click where you hear a sound. A real sound has a source you can find. A phantom does not — it is the session still playing in your head.

The experience I want to create. Arrive in calm. Wonder at small real sounds. Doubt when sounds come from empty space. Notice that every click makes the room louder. Leave by choosing to stop — and hearing, for the first time, actual silence.

Arrive
A dark plan of the room, a REC light, a 30-second clock. Ordinary.
Wonder
Small real sounds — a cymbal settling, a stand creaking — each with a place.
Doubt
Fragments of the session come from places where nothing stands.
Change
Clicking phantoms makes them multiply. Attention feeds the hallucination.
Leave
The player stops clicking. The phantoms fade. The take is finally clean.
Carry away
Silence is not empty. It is what the room sounds like when someone is listening.

06

Style & sound guide

The rule for every choice: the eye should always be a little behind the ear. The picture stays minimal so the player has to listen.

Visual & interface

Composition. A top-down floor plan of one room, drawn in thin lines, like an engineer’s session sketch. Objects are outlines, never filled, so a phantom outline and a real object look almost the same — the player cannot settle the question by looking.

Colour. Two signals only. Cold blue means a sound had a source. Hot pink, dashed, means it did not. Everything else stays in the near-black and grey of my portfolio.

Interface. Only what a real recording screen shows: a REC light, a 30-second clock and one level meter. No menu, no score, no text during play except the opening line.

Motion. Real sounds draw smooth rings at full frame rate. Phantoms draw at a stuttering 12 frames per second, a difference you feel before you can name it.

Sound, silence & rhythm

Position is the information. Every sound runs through a Web Audio stereo panner and a low-pass filter set by its distance, so the ear can locate it. This is the whole skill the game teaches.

Real sounds are dry and close. Short, physical sounds from the end of a session — a cymbal settling, a stand creaking, a chair, the air conditioning clicking off.

Phantoms are wet and far. Fragments of the session itself — a breath before a vocal line, one piano note, a count-in — with long reverb, so they sound remembered rather than present.

Silence is the reward. The ending removes the music, not adds it. The last thing the player hears is the room’s real hum.

Sources. All audio will be my own recordings from ASU Fusion Studio, so there are no licensing problems. Sound starts off; the visitor turns it on.

07

Two-week build plan

Week 2 · Build

Playable prototype

  1. Core loop first. Canvas room plan, one click handler, Web Audio panner + low-pass by position. Test that a sound can be located on headphones.
  2. Sound assets. Record and edit room tone, 4–6 real sources and 6 phantom fragments at Fusion Studio.
  3. Stages as a state machine. Opening → Calibrate → Phantoms → Feedback → Ending, each with one rule change.
  4. The ending. 10-second idle timer, phantom fade-out, playback of the “clean take”.
  5. Basics. Sound toggle (off by default), headphone prompt, touch support.

Done when: a stranger can play from opening to ending without me explaining anything.

Week 3 · Test & refine

Playtest & publish

  1. Playtest with 5 people — 3 audio students, 2 non-audio. Watch silently, then ask what they thought was real.
  2. Measure two things: can players locate real sounds in stage 1, and do they discover that stopping is the way out.
  3. Tune panning width, phantom reverb, stage length and the idle-timer hint.
  4. Accessibility: a visual-cue mode (direction arrows at the edge) for players without headphones or with hearing differences.
  5. Publish on this portfolio with a short process page and playtest notes.

Risks: laptop speakers make left/right weak → visual-cue mode; players may never stop clicking → after 60 s the REC light starts to slow down as a hint.
